# Teach the app the instructors' playbook

**Local schools in northern Fuerteventura don't compute a score. They run four gates in order: is there swell from the right direction and size for each coast, is the wind offshore or light *over the water* at that spot, which part of the tide works for that reef at that swell size, and which spot suits each group's level. Then they put the session in the hours where those gates overlap, usually the morning, before the north-east trade wind goes onshore.** On 14 November 2026 that logic sent schools to La Caleta around 11:00. It was just after low water (10:01). A NNW swell (the direction this bay needs) was running. And at sea the wind was still cross-onshore NE, before the models turned it onshore N–NNE at about 13:00. The app picked 13:00 for two reasons, both of which pushed in the same wrong direction. It reads wind from an **inland grid cell near Lajares**, because Open-Meteo picks a land cell by default; that cell showed the wind easing and staying NE. And it applies a fixed `'mid-high'` tide preference that most of the evidence doesn't support. The forecast sites rated it 0/10 because their automated ratings use swell size, period and wind only, with no tide and no local rules. To behave like a local expert from day one, the app needs three things that no amount of personal session-learning can replace. First, a **written rule base for all 21 spots**: conditional tide windows, wind vetoes, size gates that refer to other spots, level suitability and fallbacks, each rule carrying its source and a confidence. Second, **wind taken from sea cells across several models**, with their disagreement shown as confidence. Third, a **validation loop** that backtests the rules on Open-Meteo archives and checks them against labelled observations. One limit has to be stated plainly: the schools' daily choices live in Instagram stories, WhatsApp groups and instructors' heads, and **only one fully dated school choice (yours) could be found**. Schools teach the rules, but checking them against what the schools actually do will be slow.

## How to read this report

Three kinds of evidence appear throughout. **Measured** means an observation: your session report, or a station record. **Model** means numerical forecast or analysis output, mostly from Open-Meteo. **Inference** means reasoning from the sources, not a statement any source makes. For 14 November nothing about the waves or wind was measured by an instrument. Your observation is the only first-hand record, and the tide table is an astronomical prediction, which is reliable for timing. No north-coast weather station with a live record was found. Windfinder's Corralejo station reported only from July 2011 to October 2012 ([Windfinder Corralejo](https://www.windfinder.com/windstatistics/corralejo)), and the nearest listed stations are the airports. The spot rules come from published guides. Their authority varies a lot, so every rule below carries a source class and a confidence.

## Schools decide with four gates, not a score

### Where the knowledge comes from

No school in the north publishes its decision procedure. What they do publish is consistent. Surfhouse Point Break says the start time and place of each lesson "will be decided on the basis of the conditions of the day (tide, direction of the swell, wind direction)" ([Surfhouse listing](https://api.surfholidays.com/accommodation/canary-islands/fuerteventura/surfhouse-point-break-watersports)). Soul Surf picks the spot the day before from the forecast ([Soul Surf listing](https://register.surfholidays.com/surfschool/canary-islands/fuerteventura/soul-surf-fuerteventura)). Kailua confirms the start time the day before according to the tide ([Kailua Surf School](https://www.kailuasurfschool.com/)). Flag Beach runs a mobile school that "each morning" picks "the best of 10+ beaches" between Corralejo and El Cotillo and chooses "the beach that suits each group" ([Flag Beach](https://www.flagbeach.com/sport/surf/)). At LaPoint the group gathers around the forecast to talk through tides and wind and pick spots on the map ([LaPoint blog](https://www.lapointcamps.com/blog/a-day-in-the-life-at-lapoint-fuerteventura/)). A Planet Surf Camps guest wrote that class times "changed every day depending on the tides and waves", and that her beginner group surfed Playa del Moro while other groups went to Rocky Point ([Greta's Travels](https://gretastravels.com/surfing-in-fuerteventura/)).

The inputs are ordinary. Schools refer to surf-forecast.com, which gives hourly wind state and tides and updates every 6 hours ([surf-forecast Fuerteventura](https://www.surf-forecast.com/regions/Fuerteventura)). They also use Windguru, which covers the Canaries with a 9 km WRF model ([Windguru help](https://www.windguru.cz/help.php)), Windfinder's 5–7 km Superforecast ([Windfinder](https://www.windfinder.com/weatherforecast/fuerte_mistral)) and multi-model pages such as Meteoblue's ([Meteoblue multimodel](https://www.meteoblue.com/en/weather/forecast/multimodel/fuerteventura_spain_2517481)). Beyond that they look at the water: FuerteventuraPlayas runs webcams at Corralejo Bay, El Campanario, Bristol and Rocky Point ([FuerteventuraPlayas surf report](https://www.fuerteventuraplayas.com/en/fuerteventura-weather/corralejo-surf-report/)). Flag Beach shows a live conditions panel with flag status, wind, tide, swell and water temperature ([Flag Beach](https://www.flagbeach.com/sport/surf/)). **What separates them from a forecast site is not better data. It is a set of per-spot rules built up over years, applied to ordinary forecasts and corrected by looking at the sea.** That is good news for the app, because rules can be written down.

Schools also work under constraints you don't have. They need water that is safe for beginners. They split groups by level, which is why Flag Beach picks a beach "that suits each group". And they work within beach hours: one listing gives 10:00–17:00 from October to March ([Flag Beach camp listing](https://www.surfcamp-online.com/en/surfcamps/viewproperty/flag-beach-surf-camp-corralejo-fuerteventura/88)). So a school's choice tells you the spot was *suitable for that group's level at that hour*. It does not prove the spot was the *best wave* on the island. That difference shapes how school choices can be used as ground truth (see the validation section).

### The decision order the rules imply

Put the published spot rules together and a clear order emerges. This order is inference, but it is consistent with every school statement found.

**Gate 1 is swell, per coast.** The North Shore reefs need NW–N winter groundswell. The west coast at El Cotillo takes W–NW swell. The east-side beaches need N–NE swell or windswell, or a very large N swell wrapping round. If the North Shore is too big for a group, schools move to sheltered spots such as Rocky Point or Majanicho's inside ([Planet Surf Camps](https://planetsurfcamps.co.uk/surf-camps-canary-islands/surf-camp-fuerteventura/surfspots-fuerteventura)). **Gate 2 is wind relative to each coast, and it works as a veto.** The North Shore needs E, SE or S wind, and Planet calls easterly and southerly winds "necessary for these spots" ([Planet Surf Camps](https://planetsurfcamps.co.uk/surf-camps-canary-islands/surf-camp-fuerteventura/surfspots-fuerteventura)). El Cotillo needs east wind. Corralejo's east-side beaches tolerate N–NW wind. **Gate 3 is tide, and it sets the time rather than the spot**, as Kailua's tide-dependent start times show. **Gate 4 is level**, which decides which group goes where. Timing is then the overlap of gates 2 and 3, biased to the morning. A North Shore guide states the pattern directly: "Trade winds are mostly side-offshore in the morning, side-onshore in the afternoon" ([fuerteventurasurfschool.com](https://fuerteventurasurfschool.com/blog/197-surfing-north-shore-fuerteventura-the-complete-guide)). Pure Surf Camps says some reefs are clean "in the morning or when the wind eases" ([Pure Surf Camps](https://www.puresurfcamps.com/en/blog/fuerteventura-surf-guide/)).

**The app does the opposite.** It multiplies four factors into one score per spot and hour (`quality()` in `src/lib/rating.ts`), and wind costs at most about 85% of that score. Each spot is rated on its own (`recommendSpotDay` per spot, sorted in `Explore.tsx`), so a rule like "Rocky Point only when the North Shore is too big" cannot be expressed. Tide can override everything else, because a single sparse preference (`fTide`, floor 0.3, slope 2.2) multiplies the whole score.

La Caleta faces 345°. Wind from 30–40° there is cross-onshore, and wind from 0–15° is close to straight onshore. **Over the sea, every model in the table except the smooth AIFS turns the wind onshore between 13:00 and 14:00, and ECMWF, ARPEGE and GFS strengthen it through the afternoon. The land cell keeps it cross-onshore and easing.** At the same time the tide rose about 0.55 m between 11:00 and 13:00 (model sea level), while the swell height fell only about 3%. So the schools' 11:00 choice satisfies three rules at once. The spot needs a northerly swell, and gonna.surf says "North-western swell doesn't wrap into it" ([gonna.surf](https://gonna.surf/fuerteventura/la-caleta/essentials-spot-guide)). It is the last hours before the onshore turn. And it is lower water on a small, short-period swell. **One day cannot separate the wind explanation from the tide explanation (inference), and the rule base should hold both rules with their own sources rather than pick one.**

There is one awkward fact. A second wind pull, from the Historical Forecast endpoint at another inland cell (28.70, −13.85), showed the wind *backing* to ENE (66–72°) in the afternoon instead of veering N ([Historical Forecast API](https://historical-forecast-api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast)). Your report ("NE turning NNE") and surf-forecast's onshore label agree with the sea cells, but this is still a warning. **On this coast the choice of grid cell can flip the sign of the afternoon wind shift.** That is why the app needs several models, explicit sea points and a confidence measure, and not just a different default.

The forecast sites' 0/10 is no mystery either. Surfline's automated rating "uses surf height and wind conditions", and its own documentation admits it can miss tide effects at tide-dependent spots ([Surfline, Apr 2025](https://www.surfline.com/lp/whatsnew/releases/apr-25)). surf-forecast's stars come from swell size and period and are cut by onshore wind ([surf-forecast FAQ](https://www.surf-forecast.com/pages/faq)). A swell of about 1.2 m at 8 s mean period under NE-to-N wind scores near zero in both systems. A local who knows that this bay works for an hour or two around low water on a northerly swell, before the wind turns, sees a lesson window where the generic rating sees none.

## Twenty-one spots, three kinds of rule

### How much to trust each source

The guides are not independent and not equally local. vvanat.fr reproduces Planet Surf Camps' text almost word for word ([vvanat.fr](https://vvanat.fr/blog/carte-spot-surf-corralejo/)). surf-forecast's tide lines are templates; its Bristol page says "The quality of the surf isn't affected by the tide" ([surf-forecast Bristol](https://www.surf-forecast.com/breaks/Bristol)). The most local and most conditional statements come from Spanish-language sources: Jandía Riders (Fuerteventura bodyboarders), surfmarket.org, singlequiver and Surf Skate Academy FTV. Next come detailed first-hand Wannasurf entries, then the FuerteventuraPlayas portal (structured fields from a local webcam operator, with some errors), school pages, travel guides, and finally aggregators. The ranking is my own judgement, but it rests on a clear test: **a source that states a condition ("lower tide = more powerful and dangerous", "holds size above all at high tide") outranks a one-word "best tide" label.** Single-word labels often encode visitor safety (high tide = more water over the reef) rather than wave quality.

Three rule types recur across all three coasts. **Size-dependent tide windows:** shallow lava reefs get steeper and more dangerous as the tide drops, and on small swells a rising tide can shut them off. **Wind vetoes per coast.** **Cross-spot gates:** "works only when the North Shore is too big." A table of single attributes cannot hold any of these, and the app's `Spot` type is exactly such a table.

### The playbook between spots

The most valuable knowledge is relational, and the app has none of it today. When the North Shore is too big, the sources send you to Rocky Point inside ([Planet](https://planetsurfcamps.co.uk/surf-camps-canary-islands/surf-camp-fuerteventura/surfspots-fuerteventura); [Wannasurf comment, 2007](https://en.wannasurf.com/spot/Europe/Canary/Fuerte_Ventura/rocky_point/index.html)) or into Majanicho bay at mid to high tide ([FuerteventuraPlayas](https://www.fuerteventuraplayas.com/es/spot/majanicho-inside/)). Rocky Point outside comes in at about North Shore +5 m. When the north is maxed out, "the swell wraps around the island and the other spots start working" ([Surf Skate Academy FTV](https://surfskateacademyfuerteventura.com/surf-en-fuerteventura/)). At El Hierro the left works first, the right needs more and The Bubble more still ([Wannasurf Derecha](https://en.wannasurf.com/spot/Europe/Canary/Fuerte_Ventura/derecha_de_los_alemanes/index.html); [Wannasurf Bubble](https://en.wannasurf.com/spot/Europe/Canary/Fuerte_Ventura/the_bubble/index.html)). When the swell is small and from the north, Generosa still has a wave. A 2005 Wannasurf comment reported a wave there with no swell anywhere, "not even Rocky Point" ([Wannasurf Generosa](https://en.wannasurf.com/spot/Europe/Canary/Fuerte_Ventura/la_hennerosa/index.html)). In summer, with NE trades blowing, the sources point to the east-side beaches for windswell and to Cotillo, where the "offshores are at opposite angles" ([Surf Atlas](https://thesurfatlas.com/surfing-in-canary-islands/corralejo-surf/)). Beginners go to Cotillo's whitewater, Playa del Moro, El Burro and Flag Beach. Improvers go to Punta Blanca, Majanicho inside, La Caleta and Rocky Point inside ([Mondo Punta Blanca](https://www.mondo.surf/surf-spot/punta-blanca/guide/2367); [Mondo Majanicho](https://www.mondo.surf/surf-spot/majanicho/guide/2399)).

One numeric pattern supports these roles, though it is model-derived. surf-forecast's best-month "too small" shares rank how much swell each spot needs: Rocky Point outside 75%, El Muelle 33%, Bristol 19%, Majanicho 14%, Izquierda 8%, the exposed reefs 2–5% ([surf-forecast El Muelle](https://www.surf-forecast.com/breaks/El-Muelle); [Bristol](https://www.surf-forecast.com/breaks/Bristol); [Los Alemanes](https://www.surf-forecast.com/breaks/Los-Alemanes)). **That ranking is a ready-made prior for a per-spot shelter factor (inference).** The app needs that factor, because its breaking-height model treats every sample point as open ocean.

What no source gives is equally important. **No source states a swell-period threshold or a wind speed in knots for any spot.** Wind limits, period requirements and the exact size at which a tide window shifts must therefore enter the app as named parameters with priors and be tuned by validation, never presented as local fact.

## The app reads wind from a field near Lajares

### Wind over the sea, from several models

The fix starts with the request. Ask for a point 1–3 km seaward of each coast with `cell_selection=sea`, name the models explicitly, and record which grid cell each model returns. On 14 November, `sea` and `nearest` returned the ICON-EU cell 28.751, −13.951 for the north coast, where `land` had returned the inland 28.691, −13.943 ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs); [Forecast API](https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast)). Some models covering the Canaries returned data. These were ICON-EU and ICON global, ECMWF IFS 9 km and 0.25°, AIFS, GFS, ARPEGE Europe and World, UKMO global 10 km, GEM, CMA and JMA. AROME France and France HD, ICON-D2, KNMI and DMI HARMONIE, and ICON-2I returned "No data is available for this location". GFS025, GraphCast, BOM ACCESS and KMA returned nulls. **The best-resolved models available are therefore 7–10 km. No convection-permitting model reaches this coast through Open-Meteo.** AEMET runs HARMONIE-AROME with a Canary Islands domain, but that is background knowledge and was not verified in this research. It would be the next data source to check. In practice, use ECMWF IFS 9 km as the primary model for wind direction timing, with ICON (`icon_seamless`), ARPEGE Europe, GFS and UKMO as the comparison set. Report the spread of direction at the target hour as a confidence measure. AIFS and JMA were smoother on 14 November and missed the veer.

Model grid cells must also be validated, not trusted. UKMO's "sea" cell, 28.688, −13.922, lies over the island interior. In another test, `land` at 28.75, −13.90 returned an inland cell near the east side ([Forecast API](https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast)). The response's `elevation` field is the height of the requested point, not of the grid cell, so it cannot be used to detect land cells. **The app should store every model's returned grid coordinates. It should reject a cell that lies on the wrong side of the coastline, or more than about 10 km from the spot, and say so in the Info tab (inference).** No Open-Meteo model resolves the acceleration through the Bocaina strait, the wind shadow of Lobos or a local sea breeze. Lobos covers 4.5 km², less than one grid cell. Per-spot corrections for those effects can only come from observations.

### What the marine models can and cannot tell you

The wave side is better, but with known limits. In Open-Meteo, MFWAM at 1/12° has the best coastal grid, but it exposes only mean periods (Tm01) for its swell trains. ECMWF WAM 9 km provides peak period. GFS-Wave 0.16° provides swell trains with peak periods ([Marine API source table](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/marine-weather-api/+page.svelte)), which is why the app already combines all three (`marineHour` in `openMeteo.ts`). The Marine API accepts `wind_speed_10m` but returned null here, so it is no source for wind. Sea level comes from SMOC at 0.08°. Its "Accuracy at coastal areas is limited", and its datum is mean sea level, not chart datum ([Marine API docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/marine-weather-api)). For tide phase it is adequate: its model low on 14 November was within about 25 minutes of the Corralejo table. Coarse wave models also carry biases near islands that they don't resolve. One study found under 10% mean positive bias in Hs on an open 1.5° grid, but more than 30% in island-rich areas ([Mentaschi et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10236-020-01410-3)). That is another reason to learn a per-spot size correction *before* tuning any threshold that depends on size (inference).

Sample points need the same scrutiny. `samplePoint()` in `src/lib/geo.ts` moves 2.5 km from the spot along `facing` and rounds to 0.01°. For Flag Beach (facing 55°) that lands at about 28.74, −13.82, on or next to Lobos. MFWAM then served a cell about 11 km east, across the Bocaina strait. The same function currently supplies the wind point, and the wind point needs a different logic: it should be over open water on the *upwind* side of each coast, not just in front of the break.

## Rules with provenance, vetoes and a regional view

### A rule base, not a bigger table

The research found **no public surf platform that stores conditional spot knowledge.** Surfline exposed per-factor "optimal" flags ([meta-surf-forecast](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fswrobel%2Fmeta-surf-forecast)). Surfnerd rates wind, swell and tide separately per hour and weights models per spot ([Surfnerd](https://www.surfnerd.com/nl/surf-forecast/the-fort)). An open-source Open-Meteo rater uses weighted bands, vetoes and per-level size ceilings, but ignores swell direction and tide ([glama surf MCP](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/f1meabh493)). The closest precedent outside surfing is avalanche forecasting. There, explicit expert rules on top of data raised a cross-validated hit rate from 63% to about 70% ([Schirmer et al. 2009](https://www.wsl.ch/fileadmin/user_upload/WSL/Mitarbeitende/schweizj/Schirmer_etal_Statistical_forecasting_2009.pdf)), and a model mirroring "a pragmatic expert's decision-making" beat a data-only model, roughly 70% against 60% ([Schweizer & Föhn](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-7091-9343-3_23)). Rule formats are a solved problem in business software. json-rules-engine expresses nested `all`/`any`/`not` conditions and fact-to-fact comparisons ([json-rules-engine](https://github.com/CacheControl/json-rules-engine/blob/master/docs/rules.md)). DMN decision tables offer hit policies that make overlaps explicit; "Unique" is the easiest to check for completeness ([Camunda](https://docs.camunda.io/docs/components/best-practices/modeling/choosing-the-dmn-hit-policy/)).

The design below is a proposal (inference) that adapts those patterns to this app. Each spot keeps its base attributes (`facing`, swell window, size range, break type), and a new rule layer sits on top. Every rule is one of six kinds. A **tide table** keyed on swell size band (and optionally level) outputs a preferred tide range and a softness. A **veto** sets quality or suitability to zero. A **cap** limits quality. A **gate** requires a regional fact, such as "North Shore exposed swell ≥ 2.5 m". A **redirect** emits "if not here, then there". A **timing rule** penalises an hour when a veto or cap will start within the next 60–90 minutes. Every rule carries provenance: its sources with class (local-ES, first-hand UGC, portal, school, travel, aggregator) and URL, a confidence, a status (draft, active or deprecated), its parameters with a prior and a fitted value, and a validation record (`n_obs`, metric, last backtest date). Two quantities stay separate. **Quality** is level-independent: how good are the waves at this spot and hour. **Suitability** is per level: is it safe and useful for this surfer. Fun-for-you is quality × suitability. This separation already exists in the app (`quality()` vs `suitability()`), and it is what lets "low tide at Bristol is for experts" sit in suitability instead of distorting Bristol's quality.

La Caleta in this format, with its parameters marked as priors:

```ts
{
  spotId: 'fv-la-caleta',
  rules: [
    { id: 'caleta-swell-dir', kind: 'cap', when: { swellDirDom: { outside: [320, 20] } },
      then: { qualityMax: 3 }, conf: 'M',
      sources: ['gonna.surf: "North-western swell doesn\'t wrap into it"', 'surf-forecast Mechihonas: best NNW'] },
    { id: 'caleta-tide-by-size', kind: 'tideTable', key: 'regionNorthHs',
      rows: [ { lt: 1.8, tide: [0.0, 0.5], prefer: 'rising', softness: 0.6 },   // prior: gonna.surf + 14 Nov + reef physics
              { gte: 1.8, tide: [0.2, 0.9], softness: 0.7 } ],               // prior: Planet/Mondo "mid–high", untested
      conf: 'L', sources: ['gonna.surf', 'Planet', 'FuerteventuraPlayas', 'Billabong', 'Mondo', 'session 2026-11-14'] },
    { id: 'caleta-onshore', kind: 'cap', when: { windRel: 'onshore', windKn: { gt: { param: 'onshoreMaxKn', prior: 8 } } },
      then: { qualityMax: 2 }, conf: 'M', sources: ['Planet: E/SE/S wind necessary', 'Mondo: SE–SW'] },
    { id: 'caleta-turning', kind: 'timing', lookaheadMin: 90, trigger: 'caleta-onshore', penalty: 0.3 },
  ],
  levels: { beginner: 'ok-if-small', improver: 'good', intermediate: 'good' },
}
```

Three design choices carry most of the weight. **Conflicting sources produce soft rules, not hard ones.** A rule marked `L` can cost at most about 35–40% of the score, while a hard veto is reserved for `H` safety rules and onshore wind (inference). That is exactly what the app's single `'mid-high'` value got wrong: it turned a weak prior into a ×0.34 penalty. **Rules can refer to a regional context.** Each hour the engine computes exposed-swell height and direction at fixed reference points for the north, west and east coasts, plus sea-cell wind per coast. That lets "Majanicho inside needs the outside reefs at 2 m+" and "Rocky Point when the North Shore is oversized" be written as rules. **Conflict resolution is ordered:** vetoes first, then caps, then multiplicative adjustments, then redirects. A static check should flag size-by-tide combinations that no rule covers and overlapping rules with contradictory outputs (inference, following DMN practice). Use ramps rather than hard steps at band edges, so that a 5 cm model change can no longer flip an hour from "Right in your range" to zero, as happened on 14 November.

### Ranking that answers "where, when, and if not, where else"

Ranking across spots maps onto discrete-choice models from recreation economics. In these, the choice of site depends on its attributes, including weather ([conditional logit](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/104616/files/jaae319.pdf)), and the decision splits into "go or not" and then "which site" ([nested logit, CENREP](https://cenrep.ncsu.edu/cenrep/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/WP-2015-015.pdf)). The app's output should follow the same two levels. First: is there a session worth having today, for this level? Then: the best spot and window, a ranked list with the rules that fired, and explicit alternatives ("after 12:30 La Caleta goes onshore: Bristol is sheltered from W wind but expert-only; for your level, the east side is cleaner in N wind"). A window should be offered only if it holds for a minimum duration, borrowing the "minimum duration" idea from wind-sport alerts ([Windy.app](https://windy.app/blog/how-to-use-wind-alerts-for-outdoor-activities.html)). When the models disagree on the wind turn, the answer should say so: "go early; the models disagree on when the wind turns".

## Checking the rules without the schools' diaries

### The honest limitation

The research set out to collect at least 30 dated school spot choices and found **one fully dated example (your 14 November session) and two partly dated ones** ([Greta's Travels](https://gretastravels.com/surfing-in-fuerteventura/); [Waves'n'Backpack](https://wavesnbackpack.com/en/surfing-on-fuerteventura/)). A Facebook page called "Fuerte Surf Report" describes itself as a daily north-shore report, but its posts can't be read without logging in ([Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/Fuerte-Surf-Report-140267382664506/)). Schools post in Instagram stories and WhatsApp, which are neither indexed nor public. The app's own rules also forbid fetching from Surfline or surf-forecast (decision 0014 in `context/decisions/`). **The rule base therefore has to be built from the written guides, and it cannot be trained on school behaviour scraped from the web.** Checking it against what schools actually do will happen observation by observation. That is not a reason to fall back on "learn from your sessions". It is the reason to make the written rules good from the start and to treat every later observation as a test of them.

### Backtesting on archives

The archives are enough for a first backtest. The Historical Forecast API stitches the first hours of each operational run into an hourly series from about 2022. It is "Not suitable for long time series due to model version changes". The Previous Runs API supplies forecasts at fixed lead times, so a rule can be tested as it would have performed the evening before, which is how schools use it ([Historical Forecast API docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)). On the wave side, MFWAM goes back to October 2021, GFS-Wave 0.16° to October 2024 and ECMWF WAM 9 km to November 2025, and ERA5-Ocean covers 1940 onwards at 0.5° ([Marine API docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/marine-weather-api)). Whether the historical endpoint honours `cell_selection=sea` for every model was not verified, so test that first. A backtest without labels can still check that the rule base reproduces the guides' own statistics. For example, Rocky Point outside should be "too small" on most December days, as surf-forecast's 75% suggests, and Majanicho inside should be dormant whenever the exposed North Shore is under 2 m. It can also produce "golden cases" as unit tests: 12 November's WNW 2.5 m swell must not put La Caleta on top, and 14 November must favour La Caleta at 10:30–12:00 over 13:00–14:00 once sea-cell wind is used.

### Labels you can actually collect

Four sources of ground truth are realistic. Your **sessions** (fun rating and observed size) remain the richest, but they are biased toward times you chose to go. A **one-tap "check" log** fixes that. At any spot, at any time, you record size (including flat), surface (clean, textured or blown out), and whether schools are in the water and roughly how many. Flat or blown-out checks are the labels the app lacks today; `calibratedGain` even skips sessions marked flat. **School presence**, logged as part of a check, becomes the closest thing to a school-choice dataset. It should be read as "suitable for lesson groups", not "best wave". **Webcams** at Rocky Point, Bristol, El Campanario, Majanicho and Cotillo, plus FuerteventuraPlayas' YouTube livestreams ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@fuerteventuraplayas/streams)), allow manual tagging after the fact. Automated break detection from shore cameras exists in research ([Atkin & McIntosh](https://icce-ojs-tamu.tdl.org/icce/article/view/12664)) but is out of scope for a local-first app. Asking schools directly, or having a logged-in person read their stories, would give the best labels. Both were outside this research's constraints.

### Metrics and guarding against one memorable day

Report several metrics, each per spot and per season, and always against a baseline: the current engine, and a swell-plus-wind rating like the forecast sites'. **Top-1 and top-3 agreement** with logged school presence and **mean reciprocal rank** of the chosen spot measure the cross-spot choice. **Hour-level hit rate or Brier score** for "surfable" against checks and sessions measures timing. **Go/no-go accuracy** measures whether the app says "no session today" when nobody surfs. A decision tree predicting lifeguard flag colour from wind and waves is a close analogue for that last one ([NHESS 2019](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/19/2541/2019/nhess-19-2541-2019.pdf)). Tuning should change *numbers*, not structure. Fit named parameters (onshore knots, tide band edges per size, shelter factors) by maximum likelihood against the labels, regularised toward the expert prior (inference). Require a minimum number of labelled days per parameter and per regime (about 10 is a reasonable floor) before any value moves. Use leave-one-season-out validation. Write fitted values back into the rule file with a changelog entry. That is the McClung-style "data plus degree of belief" approach, and it matches your own position: **one session never rewrites a rule, but it adds a data point and can mark a rule as disputed.**

## Conclusion

The gap between the app and the schools was never about better forecasts. The instructors use the same public models you can reach. What they add is structure: gates in a fixed order, wind read for the water in front of each reef, tide treated as a matter of *when* rather than *where*, and a mental map of which spot takes over when another fails. That structure fits in a few hundred lines of rules with sources attached, and it can be written now from the Spanish local guides and first-hand reports, which say far more than the one-word labels the app copied. Where the sources conflict (La Caleta, Generosa, Flag Beach tide), the honest encoding is a soft, visible prior that the validation loop then resolves.

The 14 November case also shows that two small data defaults can produce a confident wrong answer that looks like a knowledge problem. A land-cell wind made the afternoon look calmer, and a weakly sourced tide label made it look fuller. Each error alone was survivable; together they inverted the day. The lasting design principle is the one the avalanche forecasters reached decades ago: **expert rules own the structure, data tunes the numbers, and every rule says where it came from and how often it has been right.** An app built that way can say "go at 11, before it turns onshore", and also say why.
